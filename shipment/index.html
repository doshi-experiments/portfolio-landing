Matching trucks, drivers and shipments for a Fortune 500 logistics company.

A shipment management experience that allows trucking managers to effectively manage their drivers, equipment and shipments.

Spec
Role
Lead UX Designer
Team
2 designers, 1 researcher
Duration
16 weeks
Sector
Transportation and logistics
Stakeholders
20: product owners, business analysts and tech leads
AI in the room
None then. This re-cut, yes.

The job

Planning managers plan their drivers’ day: on-time pickups, efficient routes, driver preferences, and work for every driver, all day.

  1. 1

    Keep drivers happy

    Every driver needs work, and the right work.

    Every driver has at least one load before 8:00 am.

  2. 2

    Enable planning

    Match shipments to drivers without fighting the tools.

    Quick filters, adjacent regions, and every load still to plan in one list.

  3. 3

    Provide visibility

    See what is planned, what is not, and what is about to slip.

    20 loads slipped through the cracks in a single week.

What we found

We shadowed the people doing the work before drawing anything.

  • 8 contextual inquiries

    We followed planning managers through their day to learn their tools, processes and mental models.

  • Tools from the 1980s and 1990s

    Legacy systems held together with Excel macros and Word documents as workarounds.

  • A six-week training course

    Just to use the system. So the brief became: automate everything that can be automated.

Three decisions that mattered

The product came down to three screens: shipments to cover, working drivers, and a place to match them.

  1. 1

    A split panel, not a wizard

    Type a driver’s name or pick from recommended drivers and external carriers, then see how the load changes that driver’s day before you confirm.

  2. 2

    A planning cart

    Collect the shipments you care about, multi-select them, and plan them to a driver in one move.

  3. 3

    Drivers as first-class objects

    A driver view beside the shipments, with recommended loads to fill the gaps, because the legacy system only had trucks.

“Yes, one manager can plan over 500 loads in a day.”From the original case study

From the original

Mistakes were made, lessons were learned

I’ve presented this project as a neatly packaged bundle: a concise description, a glimpse into the process, and the solution. However, there’s so much more to the story—mistakes made, numerous course corrections, and dozens of screen iterations, some so drastically different they barely resemble the final product.

The response from users exceeded our expectations.

With an AI in the room, today

What I would do differently

Synthesize the contextual inquiries in an afternoon instead of a fortnight. Build the split panel as working software and test that, not a medium-fidelity mock. Keep the listening exactly as it was.
