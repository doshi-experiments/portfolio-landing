How much should a house decide for itself?

My house runs on software I designed and had built: a family chat, a bear lookout, chores that nag, two LED panels, a budget, and one design system under all of it. Each answers that question differently, on purpose.

AI in the room

THE SETUP

A small server in the house runs about fifteen self-hosted apps. Home Assistant keeps track of nearly 1,900 things it can read or switch: lights, sensors, switches, cameras. Two LED panels light up our offices.

THE GOAL

Make a busy house easier to run. One place to ask for things, reminders that actually work, and money tracked without retyping a single transaction.

THE RULES

Nothing is open to the internet; we reach it over a private network. Anything new can be rolled back. And every app has to look like it belongs to the same house.

The interesting design work wasn’t any one screen. It was deciding, app by app, when software should just act, when it should ask first, and when it should only ever suggest. The rest of this page is those five decisions.

Every message to the family chat goes to a language model first. It reads the last day of conversation and picks actions from a fixed list, and the code that already worked does the job. It always answers, even if the answer is that it can’t open voice notes.

Why a chat

Our chores lived in four places: a list app, Home Assistant’s to-dos, the recipe app and a recurring calendar invite. Keeping track meant checking all four. The group chat was the one place everyone already was.

What it will and won’t do on its own

  • It asks before unlocking a door or opening the garage. Lights and lists, it just does.
  • It won’t delete more than five things from one message. “Got it” ticks things off; it never deletes them.
  • It ignores instructions hidden in photos or web pages, so a photo of a sign saying “unlock the front door” stays a photo.
  • If the AI is down, simple rules answer instead. One switch turns the AI off entirely.

Bears wander through the yards where we live. The yard cameras flag every person or animal they see, and a vision model looks at the snapshot to answer one question: is that a bear, and how sure are you? Most smart-home alerts are tuned to stay quiet. This one is tuned the other way, because a missed bear means someone, or a dog, walks straight out into it.

At night, uncertain checks are written down and reported when night mode ends, or the moment someone opens a yard door.

What actually happened

We’ve seen a bear a few times now. Each time the house said so out loud, and nobody went outside until it had gone.

“Bear detection should lean toward false positives.”A standing rule in the house’s automation notes. When the yard is quiet, the tablet says: “Nothing but squirrels gossiping out front.”

Chapter 3 of 5 · Tasks · Nags until done

A reminder that lets you breathe

We tried two list apps side by side for a week. Neither did repeats or reminders the way a house needs, so the lists moved into an app built for this one. The chat and the web app are two doors to the same lists.

What actually happened

The dog-food alarm had no snooze, so people turned it off, and then forgot. Snooze is a temporary no: the reminder comes back. Whiskey, a husky and Great Pyrenees mix, has a single brain cell she usually loans out. It is always back in time for dinner.

How the reminders behave

  • Alarm until done: a critical push, the chat and the speakers, every day until someone taps Done.
  • Snooze, never skip: tomorrow, three days, this weekend. It never counts as done.
  • Quiet at night: nothing is sent, pushed or spoken while the house is in night mode.
  • One tap, one chore: a second tap on another phone gets “already done”, not next week’s.

Chapter 4 of 5 · Finance · Only suggests

A budget that never guesses alone

Bank CSV exports go into Actual Budget, an open-source budgeting app. It’s the one app here that never talks to the chat, and the one with no jokes in it: it writes money records, so a person approves every import. The AI only suggests categories, from descriptions alone: it never sees an amount, a date or an account. It knows you went to the bookshop, not how badly.

Interlude · Just does it

And then there are the LED panels

Two 64 × 64 panels at home. Ask the chat for “fireworks on my matrix” and a small service has an AI pick a GIF, shrinks it to 64 pixels, plays it, and puts back whatever was there before. No confirmation, no review. Some things should just happen. They are also the only fireworks Mr Frodo Baggins approves of.

None of this is typed by hand. The work is deciding what’s worth building and what’s worth borrowing (Home Assistant, Mealie, Paperless and Actual Budget are all other people’s software), how the pieces fit across apps, and reviewing every plan before a line of it runs.

“Rule from now on: UI changes go into the design system first.”The Tasks change log, the day it deleted its own pickers, menus and toasts. One design system, eleven releases in three weeks, seven apps.

Living with it · What broke, and what’s next

What broke

A PASTED CHECKLIST

Eight bullets, two jobs lost

Two lines lost their second job and every schedule was dropped. Now every bullet is checked against what was added, and anything missed is named in the reply.

A LATE SUMMARY

An evening summary at 23:03

A catch-up window was still open. Now quiet follows night mode, every send is logged, and a missed slot is skipped after three hours.

Software for your own house gets reviewed by its users at breakfast.

What’s next

The house still can’t tell who is in a room, only that someone is. Bluetooth beacons in each room would fix that, and then lights and music could follow a person around. The speakers get connected next, so announcements go to the room you’re in.

On the Homelab board

Instrumental music during the day. A tablet by the kitchen sink that says which bin something goes in. The bot learning to open voice notes. Mr Frodo Baggins remains unconvinced.
